Overview

Each map answers one question: where in the brain do peaks fall more often than usual when a contrast measures this construct?

A task can measure very different things: in the monetary incentive delay task, gain cue > neutral cue measures reward anticipation and gain > no gain at the outcome measures reward processing. So constructs are attached to each contrast, not only to its task. They come from what the papers claim, from the Cognitive Atlas, and, where neither names one, from a language model reading the paper's own description of the analysis; all are normalized onto the Cognitive Atlas. A model of the contrasts then learns which brain networks each construct and each task draws on. No paper had to report a construct-to-coordinate mapping.

This version (November 13, 2026) tags constructs on each contrast rather than only on its task, keeps only comparisons of conditions within a task (no group or session comparisons), and gives deactivations their own author. It is built on the audited corpus: 593 papers were re-read where a contrast sat under a task or construct its peaks did not resemble, and task labels, constructs and 19 kind-of links were corrected. It uses 30 networks, chosen again on that corpus: held-out log-likelihood per peak is −9.751 at K = 30, −9.757 at K = 21 and −9.978 for the average map.

Pipeline

1

Literature per task

Each of about 220 tasks was searched by name, so a task has its full literature, not only the papers that report coordinates.

2

Construct claims

A trained text classifier finds sentences that say what a task measures, and a span tagger marks the construct named.

3

Vocabulary

Wordings are normalized into entries (98,287), merged with Cognitive Atlas concepts where they match.

4

Task profiles

Each task keeps the constructs claimed for it more often than for other tasks (at most 10 per task).

5

Contrast constructs

Each contrast gets the constructs it measures, in its direction: a task-vs-baseline contrast takes its task's; a comparison of conditions takes what papers claim for it, else a language model's attribution, else a reading of the paper; a reversed contrast takes what its baseline side has; a deactivation has its own author. Contrasts whose label merges trial phases (cue and outcome, encoding and retrieval) were re-read one by one.

6

Hierarchy

Every construct is normalized onto the Cognitive Atlas; new ones are attached under the concept they are a kind of, and missing kind-of links were filled in (add-only). Part-of links are shown but do not pool.

7

Author-topic model

Contrasts are documents, peaks are tokens; a contrast's task and its constructs are among its authors, each drawing on K brain networks and shrunk toward its parents.

8

Release

100 independent fits; each construct's map is averaged over them, with its reliability and an uncertainty band from 50 refits to resampled papers. Each task's normalized contrasts with at least 10 analyses from 5 papers also get a model map and MKDAChi2 maps of their own, with links to their analyses on Neurostore.

Claims and vocabulary

The claim finder runs without a language model at run time. An ensemble of BiomedBERT classifiers, calibrated so that a kept sentence has at least a 90% chance of being a claim, reads each paper; a second model marks the construct in the sentence. It was trained on 36 papers labelled by two people and adjudicated (9,424 units, 663 claims). Across the literature it found 309,696 claims using 125,038 distinct wordings.

Normalization turns those wordings into 98,287 vocabulary entries. It lemmatizes, drops adjective-only fragments, folds "inhibit the response" into "response inhibition" and "control of attention" into "attention control", and merges entries linked to the same Cognitive Atlas concept. It keeps apart terms that papers list side by side: executive function and cognitive control are separate, because 215 papers write "executive function and cognitive control". Every merge is recorded.

A construct joins a task when papers about that task claim it distinctively more often than papers about other tasks (a log-odds z of at least 2, in at least 5 papers), keeping the 10 most distinctive per task.

The model

The model is the hierarchical author-topic model of the task-networks project, the family Yeo and colleagues (2015) introduced for BrainMap. Each contrast is generated by its authors:

  • its constructs, what this contrast measures (on 27,107 of the 27,905 contrasts);
  • its task, what the task's contrasts share beyond their constructs;
  • its stimulus and response modality and stimulus material (auditory, button press, verbal, faces and so on), which carry how the task was run;
  • a shared author for the pattern common to every contrast, and a deactivation author on baseline > task contrasts.

Each author is a mixture over K brain networks, and each network is a map over 28,549 voxels on a 4 mm grid. A peak is explained by one of its contrast's authors, so a construct learns what its contrasts share across tasks and the task author what is left. Each mixture is pulled toward its parents (a construct toward the broader construct it is a kind of; a task toward its constructs and family), so thin authors borrow from related ones and a task the model has never seen gets a prediction from its constructs.

A construct's map is its own mass plus that of the narrower constructs below it, times the network maps: it rests on the contrasts tagged with it, never on every contrast of a task that measures it. A task's map is the mixture of its own contrasts (its author's share plus the constructs tagged on them). Modality, material and shared mass are left out: a construct is what contrasts measure, not how they were run.

The structure was chosen on held-out data. Constructs as authors of each contrast beat constructs as parents of tasks (the previous version) and a variant where each contrast is its own author nested under its task and constructs:

Stability

EM finds a local optimum, so fits from different random starts differ. Three fitting procedures were compared on the same 20 starts at K = 21, the previous release's K (the comparison was not repeated at K = 30):

Gibbs sampling followed by EM, the Yeo lab's released procedure, finds better solutions and was adopted. Even so, not every network recurs: over this version's 100 starts, 14 of 21 recur in at least 80% of fits. The data do not decide how the remaining activation divides.

Construct maps are much steadier than networks, because a construct's map sums over networks: a region split in two by one fit and kept whole by another gives the same construct map. Across the 100 release fits the median construct's probability map correlates 0.89 between fits, and 84% of constructs exceed 0.8.

Which papers were published matters more than the optimizer. Refits to 50 resamples of papers, each from a new start, give a band whose median width is 3.9 log2 units, against 1.5 across starts on the full data. Constructs now rest only on the contrasts tagged with them, so most rest on fewer contrasts than when they pooled whole tasks, and the median band is wider than the previous version's 2.9. Well-studied constructs stay tight (reward processing 0.8, motor control 0.9, working memory 1.3); thin ones widen (processing speed, 32 contrasts in 21 papers, 4.6, with none of its shown region reliably enriched).

Reading a map

  • Probability is P(peak at a voxel | the construct), shown scaled to its own maximum: where the construct's peaks fall. It is dominated by regions common to many tasks.
  • Enrichment is log2 of P(peak at a voxel | the construct) over P(peak | the average task). By Bayes' rule it is the reverse-inference map: how much a peak at that voxel raises the odds that the task measures the construct. A value of 1 means twice the average task's rate.
  • It is shown only where the construct puts at least 20% of its maximum probability, so a ratio of two tiny numbers is never drawn.
  • Reliability is the mean correlation of a construct's map between the 100 independent fits.
  • The seed band is the 2.5th–97.5th percentile of each voxel's enrichment across those fits: how much of the map depends on the optimizer. The bootstrap band refits on papers resampled with replacement and adds how much depends on which papers were published; it is the confidence band.
  • What the papers found is computed without the model, with NiMARE's MKDAChi2 (Wager et al. 2007): the construct's or task's contrasts against all other contrasts, from 10 mm spheres around each peak. The uniformity test shows where its contrasts activate consistently; the association test, where activation is more likely than in other contrasts (the reverse-inference map Neurosynth showed). Positive z at FDR q < 0.05. Where it disagrees with the model's maps, the model's networks have reshaped the pattern; at K = 30, as at K = 21, one network covers the whole striatum and peaks ventrally, so tasks with dorsal-striatum peaks get ventral-centred model maps.
  • Credited mass is how much data the map actually rests on, in contrast-equivalents: each contrast's peaks are shared among its constructs, task, modality, material and shared authors, so a construct gets only its share. Processing speed's 32 contrasts give it 6.5 contrasts' worth of evidence; reward processing's 987 give 208.

Limits

  • Many contrast tags are machine-made. Where no paper claim or Cognitive Atlas link names a construct, it was attributed by a language model or read from the paper by one. Each source is recorded, but they are not all checked by a person.
  • Trial phases are only partly separated. 1,140 contrasts whose label merged phases (cue and outcome, encoding and retrieval, regulation and instruction) were re-read one by one; about 170 more (emotion regulation, gambling decision and outcome) are still pending.
  • Thin constructs are less reliable. Reliability tracks credited mass; 208 constructs are tagged only within one task, so their maps cannot separate the construct from that task.
  • One network covers the striatum. At K = 30, as at K = 21, dorsal and ventral striatum are not separated, so constructs with dorsal-striatum peaks get ventral-centred maps; compare with the MKDAChi2 maps.
  • Claims are not ground truth. A map shows where peaks fall for contrasts said to measure the construct, so it inherits the field's usage, including loose or contested usage.
